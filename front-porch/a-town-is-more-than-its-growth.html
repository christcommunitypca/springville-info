A Town Is More Than Its Growth

One neighbor's thoughts on life, faith, and the town we call home.

November 23, 2026

I've been thinking about what makes Springville feel like Springville.

It's not an easy thing to put your finger on. We have our familiar streets, local businesses, schools, churches, and places where people gather. But none of those things, by themselves, really makes a town a community.

It's the people. Or perhaps more accurately, it's the relationships between the people.

And that's been on my mind as Springville considers its future.

The city is currently asking residents to help shape a new comprehensive plan. There's a survey asking what we think about growth, development, transportation, housing, and what we'd like our town to look like in the years ahead.

Those are important questions. But I wonder if there's another question we ought to be asking.

What kind of neighbors do we want to be?

There's plenty to debate when a small town grows. More houses might mean more families, more customers for local businesses, and more opportunities. They might also mean more traffic, greater demands on our schools and roads, and the loss of some things we've grown to love.

I suspect most of us want at least a little of both worlds. We'd like the conveniences that come with growth without losing the character that makes a small town appealing.

And there's nothing wrong with that.

But I wonder if sometimes we place too much responsibility on city planning to preserve the things we cherish about our community.

We can preserve historic buildings and still lose our sense of history. We can build beautiful parks where nobody bothers to get to know anybody. We can have wonderful schools while remaining strangers to the families whose children attend them.

And we can fight every new development that comes along without doing a single thing to make Springville a better place to live.

That's because a community is more than its buildings, boundaries, and population.

The Bible teaches something remarkable about human beings. We're created in the image of God. Every one of us. The longtime resident, the family who moved here last month, the teenager working a first job, and the elderly neighbor who's watched this town change for decades.

That ought to shape how we think about one another.

It doesn't mean we'll agree on every issue. We won't. And loving our neighbors doesn't require pretending every proposal is a good one.

But it does mean the people affected by our decisions matter more than winning an argument.

I think there's a temptation, especially when discussing change, to divide ourselves into those who belong and those who don't. Those who remember how things used to be and those who have arrived hoping to build something new.

But every longtime resident was once a newcomer, or descended from one. And every new family has the opportunity to become part of what makes Springville special.

That doesn't happen automatically.

It happens when we learn someone's name. When we support a local business. When we look after an aging neighbor. When we show up for something that benefits the town even though there's nothing in it for us.

None of those things requires a city ordinance or a comprehensive plan.

I'm glad Springville is thinking about its future. I hope residents take the opportunity to share their thoughts. Good planning matters, and the decisions we make today will affect families for years to come.

But I hope we remember something else along the way.

A growing town isn't necessarily a flourishing town.

We can have more houses, better roads, and thriving businesses and still find ourselves living among people we barely know.

Or we can make room for new neighbors while holding on to something far more valuable than a particular version of the past.

A genuine sense of community.

Maybe the best way to preserve what we love about Springville isn't simply to protect what it used to be.

Maybe it's to practice the kind of neighborliness that made us love it in the first place.

And that's something every one of us can do, starting right outside our own front door.
