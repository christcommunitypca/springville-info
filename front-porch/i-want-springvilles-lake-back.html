I Want Springville’s Lake Back

Springville once had a lake downtown. Our changing water supply and new comprehensive plan offer reasons to revisit restoring it.

November 28, 2026

Growing up in Rainbow City, I came to Springville for Decoration Sunday. My mother remembers when there was a lake downtown, but I never had the opportunity to see it.

The more I learn about it, the more I wish it were still here.

In 1883, Dr. James McLaughlin deeded the springs to the town. Residents worked with mules to excavate a lake, and in 1900 a concrete retaining wall was constructed. Large carp eventually became an attraction, with families traveling through Springville stopping to feed the fish.

While researching its history, I came across a 1961 U.S. Geological Survey record showing two of Springville’s springs producing a combined 900 gallons of water per minute. At that rate, they would produce nearly 1.3 million gallons of water every day.

God has blessed our little town with a remarkable natural resource.

What has changed since 1972?

The lake was filled in 1972 over concerns about protecting our drinking water. Springville's 2008 comprehensive plan identified the lack of a filtered water system as an obstacle to reopening the spring.

But our water supply has changed considerably.

According to the city's 2025 audited financial statements, Springville is contractually obligated to purchase 750,000 gallons of water every day from the Coosa Valley Water Supply District, regardless of whether we use it. That commitment could extend through January 2059.

Interestingly, Springville's proposed 2020 comprehensive plan acknowledged that we were purchasing more water from Coosa Valley than we were using at the time.

Our spring remains an active drinking water source, but I would like to know how much we depend upon it today and whether some of its water could be used to restore the lake without compromising our drinking water supply or our capacity for future growth.

Bringing back the lake

Former Mayor Dave Thomas proposed restoring the lake in 2020 and again in 2021. I appreciate his vision and would love to see our current mayor and city council build upon it.

There are several practical steps the city could take without initially committing substantial taxpayer funds:

  • Consult with the Alabama Department of Environmental Management about restoring the lake while protecting our existing drinking water supply.
  • Request free preliminary engineering assistance available through the Environmental Protection Agency for eligible water infrastructure projects.
  • Seek planning and design assistance through the National Park Service's outdoor recreation program.
  • Identify state and federal grants that could help fund restoration.

I would love to see the restoration of Springville Lake become one of the stated goals of our new comprehensive plan.

A lake in the heart of downtown

One of the things I appreciate most about Big Springs Park is seeing people of all ages enjoy it. I can picture families getting something to eat on Main Street and walking down to the lake, children feeding fish and older residents sharing stories about the lake they remember.

Our downtown businesses would benefit from another reason for people to spend time downtown. Our residents would gain another beautiful public space, and we would recover an important part of our town's history.

There are plenty of communities along Interstate 59 with room for new businesses, restaurants and subdivisions. How many have the opportunity to restore a historic, naturally fed lake in the heart of downtown?

I would encourage our mayor and city council to actively pursue the restoration of Springville Lake, and I hope my neighbors will express their support for it as well.

My mother remembers a Springville that had a lake. I would love for my grandchildren to know a Springville that has one, too.
