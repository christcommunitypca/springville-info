More Than a Railroad Crossing

Looking again at what Springville already has.

December 8, 2026

I've lived around Springville long enough to know that when people talk about growth, the conversation usually turns to what we don't have.

More restaurants. More shopping. More places to work. Better roads to handle the traffic.

I've been part of those conversations. I've said some of those very things myself. There are plenty of things I'd like to see come to Springville, too.

But something about the Springville Commerce Park has caught my attention. Instead of simply asking what else our town needs, somebody started looking at what we already have.

The railroad, for one.

We've had trains coming through Springville for generations. Most of us probably think about them when we're waiting at a crossing. But railroad access is also a valuable resource for industry. Combine that with Interstate 59 and available land, and suddenly Springville has something worth offering businesses that might otherwise never give us a second look.

That's the kind of thinking I appreciate.

And this isn't just an idea being discussed anymore.

Work on the roughly 240-acre Springville Commerce Park is underway. Land has already been cleared, and a $3.8 million Growing Alabama grant is helping prepare the property for businesses to move in. The effort brings together the city, county, state, and partners including Norfolk Southern.

Of course, preparing the land doesn't guarantee that companies will choose to locate here. But we're doing more than hoping new employers will come. We're making a place for them.

I like seeing that kind of initiative.

And there's another piece of this that interests me.

The county has requested funding for improvements around State Route 23 and Interstate 59, including additional lanes and a proposed roundabout.

Now, I know roundabouts aren't everyone's favorite subject.

But this particular proposal has my attention for a very personal reason.

One of my children was hit by another vehicle while traveling toward Springville on Highway 23 at the I-59 exit ramp. Another was run off the road by an 18-wheeler in that same area. Thankfully, that time there was no collision.

For our family, the difficulties at that intersection are more than a minor inconvenience. We've experienced firsthand what can happen there.

Drivers exiting the interstate can have a difficult time seeing approaching traffic on Highway 23. I don't know whether a roundabout would have prevented either of our experiences, but I certainly welcome a serious look at how to make that intersection safer.

And I appreciate something else about the approach to these improvements.

The Highway 23 bridge replacement is an important project. I applaud the willingness to take time to consider whether additional improvements should be incorporated into the larger plan.

Sometimes waiting is the wise decision.

If we have an opportunity to improve more than originally anticipated, shouldn't we take the time to consider it? Roads and bridges are investments we expect to serve our community for decades. It makes sense to think beyond the immediate project rather than discover later that we missed an opportunity.

That doesn't mean every new idea deserves funding or that every proposed change will prove worthwhile. But I appreciate leaders being willing to ask whether there's a better solution before committing to one.

The roundabout is still a proposal. It deserves careful engineering, an honest assessment of costs, and consideration of how traffic actually moves through the interchange.

But I'm glad we're asking whether there's a better way.

Of course, there are questions worth asking about the commerce park, too.

What kinds of employers will it attract? Will the jobs offer good opportunities for people who live here? How will additional truck traffic affect nearby roads? What will the improvements cost, and who will pay for them? What about the people who live closest to the development?

Those questions matter. Being excited about an idea doesn't mean giving it a free pass.

Still, I hope we don't become so accustomed to worrying about change that we lose our ability to recognize a good possibility when it comes along.

I think there's something to be said for making wise use of what we've been given.

We talk about stewardship in our homes and churches, but it applies to communities, too. We have land, roads, railroad access, and people with the ability to imagine what those resources might become. Using them wisely requires both ambition and restraint.

And I would much rather see us asking how to make the most of those opportunities than assuming our only choices are to remain exactly as we are or become something we no longer recognize.

I love the character of Springville. I don't want us to lose it. But I don't think loving our town means resisting every new idea.

Sometimes it means being willing to build something worthwhile for the people who will live here after us.

There's an old railroad running through our town. There's an interstate just down the road. And there's land being prepared to provide work for families who currently have to travel elsewhere to find it.

I think that's worth exploring.

Maybe part of Springville's future has been right here all along.
