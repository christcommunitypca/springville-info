When More Becomes the Measure

What are we really hoping sports will give our children?

December 1, 2026

I appreciate the athletic opportunities our children have here in Springville and the many people who invest in them. Having watched my own children participate in sports over the years, I've come to appreciate the lessons they learn along the way.

They learn to represent their school well in both victory and defeat. They discover what it means to be part of something bigger than themselves and how pushing beyond their own perceived limits can benefit their teammates individually and the team as a whole.

These are valuable lessons for every sphere of life, including the Church. God has given each of us gifts for the edification of the body of Christ. Our presence and active participation in the life of the Church are as much for the good of others as they are for our own.

As we pursue athletic opportunities for our children, I think it's worth examining the priorities of our families. Have our athletic endeavors begun to assume a place in our lives that belongs to God alone?

Sports can become an idol.

We don't like that word. We think of idolatry as bowing before some ancient statue. But an idol doesn't have to be made of wood or stone. It can be something good that we've allowed to become ultimate.

It begins innocently enough. Another practice. Another tournament. Another travel team. Another season. Eventually, there is no offseason. No evening left alone. No weekend that belongs to the family. Everything else must arrange itself around the demands of the sport.

And we rarely stop to ask: For what?

Perhaps a college scholarship. Perhaps the opportunity to play at the next level. Perhaps simply the satisfaction of knowing our child was the best they could be.

There's nothing wrong with pursuing excellence. And if a young person has the ability and opportunity to play college athletics, that's a wonderful gift.

But what if they don't?

What if they give their childhood to a sport and never play another game after high school? What if they do play in college, and then it's over?

What will remain?

That's a question worth asking because even the most successful athletic career eventually ends. But the life our children live before Almighty God does not end when the final whistle blows.

As Christian parents, we have a much greater ambition for our children.

We want them to know Jesus Christ. To trust him deeply. To live richly in him. To love what he loves and honor him with their lives.

We want them to stand on our shoulders and grow further into Christ than we have. To know his Word more deeply, trust his promises more confidently, and serve him more faithfully.

That's the success we ought to be praying for.

And if that's truly our desire, shouldn't it shape the decisions we make while they're growing up?

What does it teach our children when we insist that God comes first, but every practice, tournament, and game takes precedence over worship? What does it communicate when there's always time for training but rarely time for prayer, Scripture, or simply being together as a family?

I'm not suggesting that a missed Sunday service proves a family has made sports an idol. Life is more complicated than that. Nor am I suggesting that serious athletes cannot be faithful Christians.

But the patterns of our lives reveal what we treasure. Our children are watching, and they're learning what matters most long before they can articulate it.

And I wonder whether some of us have placed expectations on our children that God never placed on them.

We want them to win. To start. To be recognized. To receive the scholarship. To accomplish something we never did.

But are we equally eager for them to grow in holiness? To love their neighbor? To serve without being noticed? To remain faithful when obedience costs them something they desperately want?

Those things won't appear in a box score.

Yet they matter infinitely more.

Springville should have good athletic programs. We should support our coaches, encourage our athletes, and work to provide excellent opportunities for our children. We can ask legitimate questions about how our youth and high school programs are run.

But I hope we can also ask something harder of ourselves.

Are we raising children who love sports, or children whose lives revolve around sports?

Because those are not the same thing.

And the difference matters far beyond the playing field.

I hope our children play hard, enjoy their teammates, and experience the satisfaction of giving their best. I hope some of them have opportunities to compete at the next level.

But more than any of that, I hope they grow up knowing that their greatest purpose isn't to be an exceptional athlete.

It's to glorify God and enjoy him forever.

And no amount of athletic success can replace that.
