A Town That Still Tells the Christmas Story

One neighbor's thoughts on life, faith, and the town we call home.

December 15, 2026

Springville had a church before Alabama became a state. In 1817, nine early settlers gathered to organize what we now know as First Baptist Church. Long before we had paved streets, electricity, or even the name Springville, there were people gathering here to worship Jesus Christ.

Think about how many generations have come and gone since then. How many children have grown up hearing the story of Mary and Joseph, the shepherds in the fields, and the angels announcing the birth of a Savior?

The ways we celebrate Christmas have undoubtedly changed over those two centuries. Some traditions have disappeared, others have endured, and new ones have taken their place.

In 2008, The Alabama Baptist told of a Christmas tradition at nearby Friendship Baptist Church, established in 1858. On the Sunday before Christmas, church members received paper bags containing fruit and peppermint candy. The tradition went back further than anyone could remember.

There is something endearing about the simplicity of that celebration.

Today, just down Highway 11, Spring Valley Baptist Church has reached a decade of presenting its annual live Nativity. Members of the congregation give their time to recreate the events surrounding the birth of Jesus, inviting their neighbors to experience the story alongside them.

The Church at Bradford Road has taken a similar approach with its Walk Through Bethlehem at Homestead Hollow, bringing the sights and sounds of that ancient town into our own community.

And throughout Springville, congregations continue to tell the story through Christmas hymns, Scripture readings, children's programs and candlelight services.

I appreciate that these churches have found different ways to communicate the same truth. Some traditions are elaborate, others remarkably simple. But the value of each is found not so much in how the story is told as in the story itself.

And I hope we don't overlook the people behind these celebrations.

There are people building sets, sewing costumes, rehearsing music and preparing their churches. There are parents teaching their children Christmas hymns, grandparents explaining the meaning of the manger, and Sunday school teachers introducing another generation to the promises of God.

Much of that work will never make the local news. Yet I suspect it has shaped Springville in ways that would be difficult to measure.

We rightly take an interest in preserving our historic buildings, photographs and accounts of the people who came before us. But there is another inheritance worth preserving. Unlike an old building, it cannot be preserved simply by protecting it from the elements. It must be spoken, sung, taught and believed anew by each generation.

The Christmas story is not merely a charming account of a child born under humble circumstances. It is the announcement that God came to dwell among us, that the promised Savior had arrived, and that through him sinners could be reconciled to God.

That is a story worth telling whether we have a congregation of twenty or two hundred, a single candle or an elaborate recreation of Bethlehem.

I'd love to hear from those who have spent many Christmases in Springville. What do you remember about Christmas in our churches growing up? Were there community gatherings, Christmas pageants, caroling or other traditions that have since been forgotten?

Perhaps some of those traditions are worth recovering. Perhaps there are new ones yet to be established.

Two hundred years from now, I hope Springville will still be a town where children hear the good news that was first announced to shepherds outside Bethlehem.

And I'm thankful that it is such a town today.
